Mike Ostermiller has never run for office. His power runs through appointment, not election: a seat on the Military Installation Development Authority's board since its founding year, a registered lobbying practice built around his own law firm, and the CEO chair of a real estate trade association that has quietly outlasted a century of elected board presidents who cycle through annually. He is currently a paid consultant to the exact authority he sits on the board of.

The MIDA seat — appointed, not elected, and unusually defined

MIDA's board has seven voting seats. Six are explicitly tied to holding some other office — a mayor, a county commissioner, a state agency post. Ostermiller's isn't.

Every other seat cites a specific statutory clause tied to an official role. Ostermiller's qualification is listed simply as "Partner" — no lettered or numbered subsection at all. MIDA's own board page states plainly: "Mike has served on MIDA's Board since 2008, and he currently serves as the Board's representative to MIDA staff."

On his own MIDA disclosure form, in his own words, Ostermiller describes his role as "paid consultant / board representative with staff." Not a volunteer appointee — a paid one, on the board that decides which development projects the state approves.

The Hideout annexation bill

Before MIDA, Ostermiller was already at the center of one of Utah's most notorious land-use controversies.

2020H.B. 359, introduced as routine "Municipal Annexation Revisions." Summit County later alleged Ostermiller was heavily involved in adding language allowing a municipality to annex distant land without a petition or county consent — as long as the private property owner agreed in writing.
1st SubThe cross-county, no-consent language was not in the bill as introduced — it first appears in the 1st Substitute. Developer attorney Bruce Baird said Ostermiller brought the language to Sen. Kirk Cullimore "with an eye toward the Hideout annexation, among others."
FalloutOstermiller told press he was accountable for misrepresenting the bill's contents to its own sponsors, Cullimore and Rep. Calvin Musselman. Developers tied to the underlying Hideout project included Nate Brockbank and Josh Romney, son of Sen. Mitt Romney.
2024The Utah Supreme Court ruled in Hideout v. Summit County that Summit County lacked standing to challenge the annexation. It proceeded.

A lobbying practice built to last

Ostermiller has been a registered Utah lobbyist continuously since December 2012 — 14 years. Three of his clients have never lapsed in that entire span: the Utah Association of Realtors, the Northern Wasatch Association of Realtors, and the Utah Property Rights Coalition. His current registration lists six active clients, five of them real estate or property-rights organizations described with identical boilerplate language — "legislative advocacy and consulting" — that says almost nothing about what he actually lobbies for.

Over the full 14 years, more than 90 clients have passed through his registration — Airbnb, Apple, Uber, JUUL Labs, Pfizer, and, for two years in the mid-2010s, Blue Castle Holdings, the nuclear small-modular-reactor developer behind an early proposal near Green River. He was also a registered lobbyist for the Utah Inland Port Authority itself, MIDA's sister land-use agency, for most of 2022.

Independent, decade-old confirmation
A Feb. 12, 2012 Deseret News guest column by veteran political columnists Frank Pignanelli and LaVarr Webb named the Utah Realtors Association as having "the largest PAC in the state" and wrote plainly: "Under the leadership of Chris Kyler and Michael Ostermiller, the Realtors often prevail." This wasn't a new observation in 2026 — it was already common knowledge among Utah political insiders 14 years ago.

The chair that never turns over

Ostermiller is CEO of the Northern Wasatch Association of Realtors — a title he has held continuously since well before his lobbyist registration began in 2012, while the association's elected President position rotates through a new person almost every year. Elected officer seats there — President, Vice President, Treasurer — genuinely change hands annually through a standard succession ladder. The CEO seat does not. It's a hired staff position, appointed by the board and reviewed annually by whichever President happens to be serving that year — not voted on by the membership at all.

His 2022 reported compensation for that role: $195,000, plus $51,720 in other compensation — $246,720 total, the largest line item on the association's own tax filing.

Where the roads keep crossing

Ostermiller's own law firm — Kyler, Kohler, Ostermiller & Sorensen — pairs him with Chris Kyler, CEO of the Utah Association of Realtors, who registers lobbying clients on a nearly identical schedule under the same firm. Both men were named as co-defendants, alongside Northern Wasatch Association of Realtors itself, in a 2013 lawsuit dismissed on procedural grounds before reaching the merits.

More recently: Utah's corporate registry shows Ostermiller as director, treasurer, registered agent, and incorporator of the Nilson Foundation Inc., a nonprofit formed in July 2026 with Jed Nilson — CEO of Nilson Homes, a major Weber County developer — as its President. The two are also co-directors of a separate Ogden nonprofit, The Healing Collective Utah Inc. (Ostermiller: Director and Treasurer; Jed Nilson: President; Nilson's wife Heather: Secretary), formed the year before. Both entities' stated purposes are generic 501(c)(3) boilerplate that says nothing about what either organization actually does.

The firm itself, and a discrepancy that isn't resolved

Utah's own corporate registry, current as of a May 2026 renewal, lists Ostermiller as an active partner at KKOS. His own sworn disclosure form, signed weeks later, says otherwise.

The firm — full name Kyler Kohler Ostermiller & Sorensen, LLP — is registered not in Sandy, South Ogden, or Kaysville, where its partners actually do their work, but at an address in Cedar City, over 250 miles south, using a commercial registered-agent service. Alongside its "KKOS Lawyers" trade name, the same registration lists two others: The Entrepreneur's Law Firm, and Cedar Injury Law — a personal-injury practice name with no apparent connection to the government-affairs and lobbying work described everywhere else in this record.
