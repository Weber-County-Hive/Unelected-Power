What it is

The Utah Inland Port Authority, called UIPA, is a state agency. Its board is appointed, not elected. It works in 16 areas around Utah.

When land in those areas is built on, property taxes go up. UIPA keeps 75% of that new tax money for 25 years. The board can add 15 more years. The county, schools and other local groups get the other 25%.

On Oct. 21, 2026, UIPA's leaders spoke to a group of lawmakers. The Hive recorded it and checked what they said.

What they said, and what the records show

"We have no zoning power." True today. But the first law in 2018 did let UIPA's board overrule some city land decisions. That power was taken out.

"No new taxes." No new tax rate, that's true. But the 75% is money the county and schools would have gotten as the land grows.

"No incentives to data centers." True by UIPA's meaning: cash to a company. But UIPA lent $4.875 million, including for a power plant, to the company building the Tooele data center site. The loan was paid back.

Two numbers didn't match UIPA's own news releases.

What they didn't mention

Some of the wins they named came with UIPA money behind them: an $18 million loan to Delta, a tax rebate for A1 Lithium worth up to about $194 million, and a $5 million loan to the West Weber landowner, PCC Land. All of these are in UIPA's own public records.

Why it matters to you

Lawmakers make choices based on what agencies tell them. When the spoken numbers don't match the agency's own papers, it's worth asking which one they used.
