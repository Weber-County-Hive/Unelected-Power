CASE FILE · ORGANIZATION · HEARING CHECK · OCT. 21, 2026

The Inland Port told legislators its side. Here's what the records say.

On Oct. 21, 2026, leaders of the Utah Inland Port Authority (UIPA), a state authority run by an appointed board, presented to a legislative committee and answered a member's questions. The Hive recorded it. Each claim below is checked against UIPA's own plans, press releases, loan letters and audits.

Agency: Utah Inland Port Authority Presented: Executive Director Ben Hart · CFO Ariane Gibson Board: appointed, not elected

Posted Oct. 21, 2026 · Updated Oct. 21, 2026

New to this? Read it in plain words About 500 words, with two pictures.

The short version

  • UIPA's leaders told a committee that UIPA has no zoning power, creates no new taxes and has given no incentives to data centers. Each is true as UIPA defines it, but each leaves out something in UIPA's own records.
  • Two figures said to legislators don't match UIPA's own press releases: public safety money ($5 million said, $2.5 million in the release) and A1 Lithium's investment ("billion plus" said, about $569 million in the release).
  • UIPA's board approved a $4.875 million loan, covering a power plant, to the developer of the Tooele Valley data center campus in 2024. UIPA doesn't count loans as incentives. The loan has been paid off.
  • UIPA's own plans project $568.8 million for UIPA from three of its 16 project areas over 25 years. If all three are extended to 40 years, the Hive estimates about $910 million, and it could be higher.
  • Several successes named at the hearing came with UIPA money that wasn't mentioned, including an $18 million loan to Delta and a rebate for A1 Lithium capped at about $194 million.

Start here: what UIPA is

The Legislature created the Utah Inland Port Authority in 2018. It is run by a board that is appointed, not elected.

In its project areas, UIPA keeps 75% of the new property tax that comes from growth, for 25 years per parcel, and the board can extend that 15 more years if it finds a "significant benefit." The other 25% goes to the county, school district and other local taxing entities. UIPA uses its share for infrastructure, loans and rebates to companies. It now has 16 project areas statewide.

The words used on this page, like "tax differential," "AIB" and "PID," are explained in Words to know.

Local control
"We do not have zoning control … we do not have the right to go through permitting."
Answer to a committee member's question · Recording 8, about 0:38–1:18
True, with context left out

Matches today's law. In 2022 the Utah Supreme Court held that the inland port law "does not delegate zoning or land-use authority to the port authority." UIPA's own June 25, 2026 statement says it "does not zone property, approve land uses or determine which developments are allowed."

Left out: the original 2018 law did give UIPA's board power over land use. Its board could review "any land use decision" an adversely affected person brought to it and overrule a city decision it found "detrimental" to the port's goals. A 2018 special session narrowed that power, and it is not in today's law.

Sources: KSL on the Supreme Court ruling (June 29, 2022); Utah League of Cities and Towns letter on the 2018 special session; UIPA, "UIPA's Position on Data Centers" (June 25, 2026).

A city or county has to invite UIPA in by resolution before a project area is created.
Presentation · Recording 5 (paraphrased; see the Hive's notes on that recording)
True, with context left out

True for the newer project areas. Weber County, for example, passed resolutions in 2023 and 2024 before West Weber was created.

Left out: the first and largest area, the Northwest Quadrant in Salt Lake City, was drawn by the Legislature in 2018, not by invitation. Salt Lake City sued over the law and lost at the Utah Supreme Court in 2022.

Sources: KSL (June 29, 2022); Weber County Commission Resolutions 39-2023 and 02-2024 (in the West Weber case file).

Taxes
"No new taxes at all. It's future growth."
Answer to a committee member's question · Recording 8, about 2:37
True, with context left out

True that no new tax rate is created. UIPA keeps a share of the growth on property taxes that already exist.

Left out:

  • The 75% UIPA keeps is money the county, school district and other local taxing entities would otherwise collect when that land develops. They go without it for 25 years per parcel, or up to 40.
  • UIPA pledges its share to PIDs, and a PID can put assessments or a property tax on the land inside it. Tooele Valley's PID has issued $33 million in bonds. That is not a tax on the general public, but it is a charge on owners inside the district.
  • Part of UIPA's 75% goes back to companies as rebates, such as Lakeshore Learning's 60% for 25 years in Golden Spike and A1 Lithium's 50% (below).

Sources: West Weber, Golden Spike and Tooele Valley project area plans; Tooele Valley PID bonds (Tooele case file).

The $100 parcel that grows to $100,000 in taxes: locals get $25,000, UIPA gets $75,000, and the money stays in the project area.
Answer to a committee member's question · Recording 8, about 3:42–4:39
True, with context left out

The math is right. The split is on the growth above the frozen base, not on the whole tax bill.

"Stays in the project area" is mostly true. West Weber's own budget table also takes out $18.0 million (5%) for UIPA's administrative costs and $10.8 million (3%) for wetland mitigation over 25 years. The plan does not say where those dollars are spent.

Fair point UIPA makes: if the land would not develop without UIPA, locals get 25% of something instead of 100% of almost nothing. West Weber's land brings in about $190,000 a year in property tax today. Whether that development would have come anyway is the question readers can weigh.

Source: West Weber Project Area Plan, Appendix D budget summary.

The committee member's example: the tax is on the pipe a company makes and sells.
Committee member's description · Recording 8, about 3:09–3:26
Matches the record (as a note)

This was the member's own description, not UIPA's. UIPA's answer was about property taxes on land and buildings, not on products sold. If you quote the member, it's worth noting the difference.

Oversight
The State Auditor audits UIPA's finances.
Answer to a committee member's question · Recording 8, about 5:38
True, with context left out

True since 2023, when UIPA chose the State Auditor for its yearly financial audit.

Left out: the first one, for the year ended June 30, 2023, found two "significant deficiencies": "errors in preparing the financial statements" and "not complying with bond reporting requirements," plus weak purchasing practices. UIPA said it would fix them.

Sources: Office of the State Auditor news release (Dec. 21, 2023); UIPA's response to the findings.

UIPA has had clean state audits for fiscal years 2025 and 2026.
Presentation · Recording 5
Can't confirm

The Hive has not yet found those two audit reports. Until they are read, this is UIPA's claim.

The Legislature can audit UIPA, and it did in 2022.
Answer to a committee member's question · Recording 8, about 5:38–5:56
True, with context left out

True. The Legislative Auditor General issued "A Limited Review of the Utah Inland Port Authority," Report 2022-07, in September 2022.

What that audit found:

  • About 81% of UIPA's original contracts were sole-sourced, which the auditors said "reduces transparency."
  • Two contracts raised questions about ties between port staff and the contractors.
  • A $150 million bond was largely unspent.
  • The board needed to be more involved, including an audit committee and a board treasurer.

Sources: Legislative Auditor General, Report 2022-07; FOX 13 (Sept. 20, 2022).

The public can attend UIPA's board meetings.
Answer to a committee member's question · Recording 8, about 5:56
Matches the record

UIPA posts board agendas and minutes publicly, and plans and budgets are posted before board votes. Minutes from June 26, 2025 show members of the public attending and commenting.

Sources: UIPA Board Meetings & Agendas page; UIPA board minutes, June 26, 2025.

People can make GRAMA records requests to UIPA.
Answer to a committee member's question · Recording 8, about 6:00
True, with context left out

True, but the rules changed recently:

  • UIPA's own summary of the 2026 session says new laws added "protected records and nondisclosure agreements" provisions.
  • Since S.B. 277 (2025), GRAMA appeals go to a single director at the Government Records Office, not to the citizen State Records Committee.

Sources: UIPA, "Legislative Updates Following 2026 Session" (Mar. 28, 2026); S.B. 277 (2025).

Data centers
"The Inland Port Authority has not provided any incentives to a data center."
Ben Hart · Recording 7, about 4:26
True, with context left out

True by UIPA's own definition of an incentive as cash to a business. The Hive found no record of a cash rebate to a data center.

Left out: on April 29, 2024, UIPA's board approved a $4.875 million infrastructure loan to Zenith Bolinder, developer of the Tooele Valley data center campus. UIPA's own loan page lists its uses as water, sewer, roads and a power plant. It was a one-year loan at 3.47%, and it has been paid off.

The developer, Chuck Akerlow, told the Salt Lake Tribune his project "would not be possible without the incentives and financing provided by the port authority."

The same site also has a Love's, a hotel and warehouses, so the record shows the loan went to the data center's developer. It does not show the money went to the data center itself.

Sources: UIPA 2024 Annual Report; UIPA, "Who Qualifies for AIB Loans"; Salt Lake Tribune (Tooele case file).

"We have not had communities that have asked us to."
Ben Hart · Recording 7, about 4:44
Can't confirm

The Hive found no record of a city or county asking UIPA for data center incentives. But two project area plans the counties consented to list data centers as an industry UIPA is aiming for: West Weber and Tooele Valley (Plan Amendment #2).

"At this juncture we are not actively recruiting data centers to the state."
Ben Hart · Recording 7, about 4:44
True, with context left out

Matches UIPA's June 25, 2026 statement, which says it "is not providing financial incentives to data centers and is not actively recruiting data centers to its project areas."

Left out: the UIPA board approved Tooele Valley Plan Amendment #2 that same day. Its "Recruitment Strategy" section names data centers as one of four industries UIPA is aiming for, alongside light industrial, manufacturing and distribution.

Sources: UIPA statement (June 25, 2026); Tooele Valley Project Area Plan, Amendment #2 (June 25, 2026).

"We do have some data centers that are taking advantage of our infrastructure."
Ben Hart · Recording 7, about 5:04
Matches the record

His own statement, and it fits the Zenith loan above. He also said "infrastructure can be a benefit." UIPA's loans ran at 3.04%–3.47%. The average 30-year home mortgage was 6.76%–7.08% in September 2026, about twice as high.

"We do not enforce anything on a community."
Ben Hart · Recording 7, about 6:00
Matches the record (newer areas)

UIPA's June statement says it "supports and adheres to data center moratoriums enacted by local communities." After Tooele County paused new data center applications, UIPA said it was "not slated to consider" financing that area "any time soon — if at all" (Utah News Dispatch, Sept. 3, 2026). The Northwest Quadrant is the exception noted above.

H.B. 507 "dictates what will happen in the future … we will be in line with that legislation."
Ben Hart · Recording 7, about 6:33
True, with context left out

UIPA says this in writing too. Its June statement says it "will continue to adhere to HB 507."

Left out: following H.B. 507 doesn't come with a water limit.

  • H.B. 507's new zone chapter does not carry forward the 200,000-gallons-a-day water limit in UIPA's older law.
  • S.B. 225 (2026) changed UIPA's own rule. The fixed limit had applied only in Salt Lake City; now it applies statewide and is set by local standards, with no fixed ceiling.

Sources: H.B. 507 (2026); S.B. 225 (2026), all substitutes (S.B. 225 case file).

Projects and spending
"We've invested about $5 million into public safety on the west side of Salt Lake City. They've been able to hire about 12 new officers."
Ben Hart · Recording 6, about 3:55
Doesn't match the record

12 officers: matches. $5 million for public safety: doesn't match UIPA's own release. UIPA's April 28, 2026 release says the $5 million was split: $2.5 million for public safety and $2.5 million for Great Salt Lake wetland conservation.

Source: UIPA, "UIPA, Salt Lake City Partnership to Add Officers Serving West-Side Industrial Area" (Apr. 28, 2026).

A1 Lithium is "going to be making a billion plus dollar investment into Green River."
Ben Hart · Recording 7, about 3:06
Doesn't match the record

UIPA's own release says about $569 million.

Left out: the same release says UIPA's board approved a rebate for A1 Lithium of 50% of UIPA's tax differential for up to 25 years, capped at $193,995,683. It lists about 138 permanent jobs and 511 construction jobs.

Source: "UIPA Approves Incentive for A1 Lithium in Castle Country Project Area" (Sept. 9, 2026).

UIPA bought a historic city landfill that had been leaching outside its bounds, and found 600,000 tires it didn't expect.
Ben Hart · Recording 6, about 0:00–1:16
True, with context left out

The purchase is on record. UIPA paid $20 million to the state Trust Lands Administration for the 770-acre North Temple site. Trust Lands had bought it from the Church's Suburban Land Reserve. Salt Lake City ran the landfill from 1959 to 1979.

Left out: cleanup is estimated at $30–40 million for the first phase and possibly $150–200 million in all. Part of it is paid from the $150 million bond originally meant for a cancelled transload facility, the same bond the 2022 audit found largely unspent.

The 600,000 tires figure is Hart's statement; the Hive found no record of it.

Sources: Salt Lake Tribune (Feb. 14, 2025); UIPA release (Feb. 11, 2025).

"The Clean Ports program brought $112 million into the Northwest Quadrant."
Ben Hart · Recording 7, about 1:46
True, with context left out

The EPA awarded $112.4 million in November 2024: $110 million to the state Division of Air Quality and $2.4 million to UIPA.

Left out: UIPA's own Feb. 21, 2025 post said the grant was paused in a broader federal grant pause. The Hive could not find whether it was restored. "Awarded" matches the record; "brought" may not.

Sources: Utah Business (Nov. 4, 2024); UIPA, "EPA Clean Ports Program Grant on Pause" (Feb. 21, 2025).

About $7.5 million for Great Salt Lake shoreline buffer land with Salt Lake City.
Ben Hart · Recording 7, about 2:06
Matches the record

UIPA announced $7.5 million for land conservation on July 15, 2026 (KSL). The coverage calls it allocated; how much has been spent is not stated.

Stadler Rail is doubling its plant with UIPA's help.
Ariane Gibson · Recording 6, about 2:17
True, with context left out

True. UIPA's March 27, 2025 release says the expansion "will double the size of Stadler's existing facility" (245,000 square feet, more than $70 million, 500 to up to 800 jobs).

Left out: the terms. It's a rebate of 10% of Stadler's assessed property tax for up to 25 years.

UIPA partnered with Delta on a new air cargo facility.
Ben Hart · Recording 7, about 0:21
True, with context left out

Left out: UIPA lent Delta $18 million for the facility (KSL, Nov. 3, 2025). The share of Utah air cargo that arrives by truck came out of the recording as both "82%" and "92%," and the Hive could not confirm either, so it is not used here.

A railroad safety system to tell drivers how long trains will block crossings.
Ben Hart · Recording 6, about 4:20
Matches the record

UIPA sent Salt Lake City a $150,000 "initial contribution" for train-wait notices (KSL, Mar. 30, 2025). KSL doesn't give the system's full cost.

Helping Patriot Rail move its rail yard farther west.
Ben Hart · Recording 6, about 4:41
Matches the record

UIPA's board approved $500,000 on May 21, 2026 to help the Salt Lake Garfield & Western Railway move its East Yard to a new interchange south of the airport.

Infrastructure bank loans for a water system in Fairfield and an industrial park in Salina.
Ben Hart · Recording 7, about 3:48
Matches the record

UIPA's Feb. 9, 2026 letter to the Legislature reports:

  • Salina: $4.66 million at 3.23% for 15 years.
  • Fairfield: $1.6 million at 3.23% for 15 years.

Both have no payments for the first five years. (UIPA's news post gave Fairfield's rate as 3.13%; the letter to the Legislature is used here.)

A partnership with Weber County on a regional wastewater lift station to open up west Weber.
Ben Hart · Recording 7, about 6:52
True, with context left out

True that UIPA is a named partner (Weber County release, Apr. 28, 2026). The release gives no UIPA dollar figure.

Left out: the $5 million UIPA loan to PCC Land LLC, the West Weber landowner, for sewer infrastructure. Its history:

  • Sen. Jerry Stevenson made the motion at the UIPA board.
  • It failed 9-8-3 at the Legislature's Executive Appropriations Committee on Sept. 15, 2025.
  • It passed after Sen. Kirk Cullimore moved to reconsider.
  • UIPA's letter to the Legislature shows it was signed Dec. 15, 2025: 3 years at 3.28%.

Sources: Weber County release (Apr. 28, 2026); UIPA board minutes (June 26, 2025); EAC minutes (Sept. 15, 2025); UIPA loan letters (Feb. 9, 2026).

How much is the 75%?

UIPA's plans project 25 years. Using each plan's own budget table, here is UIPA's share and the local share for three project areas already in the Hive's files.

Estimate, labeled as such. The 40-year numbers are the Hive's estimate, not UIPA's. They assume years 26–40 collect at each area's own 25-year average pace (the 25-year total times 1.6). The extension is not automatic; the board must find a "significant benefit." The real amount could be higher, because:
  • the early years are slow while land builds out, so later years are likely above the average;
  • the clock starts parcel by parcel, so the whole area can collect for longer than 40 calendar years;
  • acres can be added (Golden Spike grew from about 1,500 to 2,140 acres, with about 1,000 more planned).

What this hearing teaches

  • Check spoken numbers against the agency's own press releases first. That's where both mismatches here showed up: public safety and A1 Lithium.
  • Listen for definitions. "We haven't given a data center an incentive" depends on UIPA's own meaning of the word.
  • Look up what each success cost. Delta, Stadler and A1 Lithium were named as wins; the loans and rebates behind them were not mentioned.
  • Notice what isn't brought up. The PCC Land loan and the Zenith loan are public records UIPA sent the Legislature, and neither came up.

Open questions

Which committee was this, and who asked the questions?

Neither is clearly said on the recording. The committee's agenda and minutes for Oct. 21, 2026 would settle it.

Were the FY2025 and FY2026 audits clean?

UIPA said so. The reports have not been read yet.

Was the Clean Ports grant restored?

UIPA's last post found says it was paused (Feb. 21, 2025).

Where are the 5% administrative and 3% wetland dollars spent?

The plans set the amounts but not where they're spent.

What is the Northwest Quadrant's projected tax differential?

Not yet in the Hive's files. It would show the statewide scale.

Questions to ask, and whom to ask

  • Your state legislators: UIPA told the committee it gave about $5 million to public safety and that A1 Lithium is investing over $1 billion. UIPA's own releases say $2.5 million and $569 million. Which figures did the committee rely on?
  • UIPA board: Do you count below-market infrastructure loans, like the $4.875 million loan to the Tooele data center's developer, when you say UIPA has given no incentives to data centers?
  • UIPA board: Why does Tooele Valley's Recruitment Strategy name data centers, when UIPA's statement the same day says it is not recruiting them?
  • Weber, Box Elder and Tooele county commissions and school boards: Will you support or oppose a 15-year extension when UIPA's board considers one, and will that be a public vote?
  • Your state legislators: Should UIPA's water standard have a fixed limit again, after S.B. 225?
  • State Auditor: Are UIPA's FY2025 and FY2026 audit reports posted, and were the 2023 findings fixed?
