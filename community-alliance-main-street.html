The Nonprofit That Runs Downtown

Community Alliance for Main Street (CAMS) runs Heber City's downtown revitalization work under a sole-source contract negotiated, approved, and signed for the City by a man who also sits on CAMS's own board. What the arrangement is supposed to buy, who actually pays for it, and what still isn't accounted for.

The Weber County Hive

Posted Sept. 14, 2026 · Updated Oct. 27, 2026

Master index Unelected Power index Matt Brower — the board seat that never came up Dallin Koecher — one line, four boards short

The short version

  • Community Alliance for Main Street (CAMS) is a Heber City nonprofit that runs downtown events and beautification under a sole-source City contract: $39,900 a year, Jan. 1, 2026 through Dec. 31, 2028, auto-renewing annually.
  • Heber City Manager Matt Brower has sat on CAMS's board since 2019. His is the only City signature on the contract, though the contract's own wording describes a Mayor's signature and a Recorder's attestation that are not on the signature page.
  • CAMS's own documents give three different totals for 2024: $193,966 (Form 990-EZ), $191,115 (Schedule A) and about $387,933 (its own balance sheet). One 2024 line, $347,465.54 for "Main Street Beautification," has no public itemization.
  • When checked, the state listed CAMS as "Active, but Delinquent" after it missed its June 30, 2026 renewal deadline. This may have changed.

What CAMS is, and why that part is normal

Cities partnering with an independent nonprofit to run downtown revitalization work is a well-established, nationwide model — not, by itself, a red flag.

Utah's own state Main Street Program, run through the State Historic Preservation Office, follows the same national framework used in over 1,200 communities across 40 states: a professionally managed downtown organization, "whether that entity is housed with a city or county, or is a stand-alone nonprofit," typically sustained by modest local government support alongside sponsorships and grants. CAMS holds Tier 2 accreditation in that statewide network.

So the basic shape of this arrangement — a city funds a nonprofit that handles beautification, downtown events, and business support — is ordinary. What follows is not a case against that model. It's an account of how this particular version of it was built and run.

CAMS's own materials answer the natural follow-up question directly: if local government already exists, why does a Main Street need its own nonprofit at all? Its stated answer is that city government manages a broad range of responsibilities and can lack a group focused specifically on a niche like a single downtown corridor. CAMS describes itself as filling that gap — not replicating the City's work, but complementing it, acting as a dedicated "specialist team" for Main Street's specific needs.

What the money is supposed to buy

Pulled directly from CAMS's own website and public materials — its stated deliverables, in its own words:

  • Beautification — flowers, hanging baskets, benches, and bike racks along Main Street; the "Flower Pot Project," 34 oversized planters
  • Heber City Historic Tour — CAMS describes itself as instrumental in launching this tour of 37 historic homes and buildings with informational plaques
  • A public water feature at City Hall — proposed by a private donor in 2018, developed with Wasatch CAPS students, completed as a CAMS/City partnership, opened June 2024
  • TAP grant-funded programming — Art in Public Spaces, Lights on Festival Street, Music on Main
  • Recurring events — Old Fashioned Christmas, Halloween Fest, and a new 2026 Cinco de Mayo event
  • Heber Market on Main (HMOM) — the one revenue line CAMS splits 50/50 with the City rather than keeping in full
  • Food-truck coordination at Main Street Park, including a proposed EV-charging project to replace gas generators at events there
  • A full-time staff position — CAMS's own May 2026 profile says it "recently secured funding for a full-time position." That position is Executive Director Rachel Kahler — a former Heber City Council Member herself, in a role the City's contract appears to fund directly

CAMS's own website lists Kahler under a page header reading "Here are just a few of the individuals who volunteer at CAMS," alongside three actual unpaid board members. Her Executive Director role is CAMS's one paid, City-funded position — describing it as volunteer service alongside genuine volunteers is a factual mismatch worth noting plainly, not a claim about anyone's intent.

Separately, CAMS's own January 2024 blog post names "Policy Influence" — engaging directly with local policymakers — as one of its four core activities. That claim predates CAMS's 501(c)(3) conversion by roughly two years, and sits in tension with the federal political-activity restrictions that came with it. See "A nonprofit that describes its own advocacy," below.

Who actually pays for it

CAMS's own materials state it does not collect membership dues from Main Street businesses. The actual cost structure runs through the City's general tax base instead:

What the City covers

  • $39,900/year direct payment, rising annually with CPI, paid quarterly in advance
  • Police services at CAMS events
  • Insurance
  • Marketing access (Ignition Creative)
  • Sound system contracts
  • Storage space in the old HL&P administration building

What CAMS keeps

  • 100% of event-sponsorship revenue, with one exception
  • Heber Market on Main sponsorships: split 50/50 with the City
  • Any additional TAP grant funding it pursues on top of the contract

Set against the deliverables list above — planters, murals, seasonal events, a modest annual calendar — the City funds nearly all of CAMS's cost of doing business while CAMS retains the revenue those same taxpayer-funded events generate. The City's own fiscal justification for the HMOM split says as much directly: staff wrote that the arrangement "should provide the City with a net increase in revenue as only a fraction of the weekly sponsorship[s] were sold in previous years" — meaning the City had been leaving that money on the table itself before handing the relationship to CAMS.

Two entities, not one

CAMS's own history page describes a continuous, 22-year organization. The state's own corporate filings tell a different story.

~2004CAMS organizes informally, founded by Tom Stone and Jeff Bradshaw following $15,000 in post-Olympics community grants — this is the basis for the "twenty-second year" language on CAMS's own site.
June 2, 2005The first corporate entity, Community Alliance for Main Street, is formally filed with the state.
October 4, 2006That entity is administratively dissolved by the state for non-compliance — roughly 16 months after filing.
2006–2019No CAMS corporate entity exists at all — a 13-year gap.
June 3, 2019A wholly new entity, Community Alliance for Main Street, Inc., is filed — the entity that holds today's City contract.
Nov. 22, 2024 (effective) / Sept. 3, 2025 (determination)The current entity is recognized by the IRS as a 501(c)(3), after — per its own history page — operating for years as a 501(c)(6) business league.
When checkedThe active entity was listed Active, but Delinquent with the Utah Division of Corporations as of when checked; this may have changed — missed its June 30, 2026 renewal deadline, updated to delinquent status July 14, 2026, after its last clean renewal on June 13, 2025.

Counted from the actual legal filings — not the informal founding story — the entity that signed the City's current contract is seven years old, not twenty-two, and its predecessor spent more of its existence dissolved than active.

On that same state filing, every director's address of record is a personal or business address — except one. Matt Brower's listed director address is 75 N Main St, Heber City: Heber City's own City Hall. The City Manager's board seat at an independent nonprofit is registered, on the state's own record, at the government building itself.

The board

Three independently-sourced records of who sits on CAMS's board — and none of them agree:

The state's own official record — the legally operative account of who actually directs this corporation — is missing three of the eight people everyone else treats as sitting board members: Ryan Fritzsche, Kelsey Berg, and Erik Rowland. Utah law (§16-6a-1607) requires nonprofits to file an annual report naming current directors, but the only consequence for missing or outdated filings is the same delinquency/dissolution mechanism already documented on this page — the law doesn't require this filing to match what a city reports or what the organization's own website says, so a three-way mismatch like this one isn't something the statute is built to catch. That makes this a real caveat, not a reason to set the discrepancy aside: state nonprofit filings only update when a renewal is filed, so this could reflect an administrative lag rather than proof those three were never properly added. Per Heber City's own staff reports, current voting members are: Tom Stone (Chair), Matt Brower, Dallin Koecher, Jeff Bradshaw (Treasurer), Ryan Fritzsche, Kelsey Berg, Erik Rowland, and Nicole Ferguson. Executive Director Rachel Kahler is staff, not a board member.

The contract

The City's Professional Services Agreement with CAMS runs Jan. 1, 2026 through Dec. 31, 2028, auto-renewing annually, awarded through sole-source procurement rather than competitive bidding. The staff report's stated justification: CAMS's history, its downtown relationships, and the cost/time of transitioning to a new vendor.

The agreement was re-noticed as sole-source with 10 days' legal notice after a complaint to the Utah State Auditor. Out of what staff called "an abundance of caution," the City solicited comparison quotes from two Park City-based nonprofits — both of which indicated the work would cost significantly more than CAMS's $39,900.

The signed agreement recites that the City "caused this Agreement to be signed by its mayor and attested by its city recorder." The actual signature block contains only one line — Matt Brower, City Manager. No Mayor's signature, no Recorder attestation. The same person negotiated the deal, approved it as a CAMS board member, authored the staff report recommending it, and is its sole named city signatory.

CAMS's own bylaws separately require formal Board approval of any "self-dealing transaction." Whether the City contract was ever reviewed under that provision is not established in any document obtained so far.

The money that doesn't add up

Three CAMS-originated documents describe the same 2024 fiscal year with three different totals:

Source2024 figure
Form 990-EZ, Line L ("gross receipts")$193,966
Schedule A, Part III ("public support")$191,115
CAMS's own internal balance sheet (total income)~$387,933

The first two are close enough to plausibly reconcile through ordinary accounting differences. The third is not — it's roughly double either federal figure, on the same year, from CAMS's own books.

CAMS's Form 990-EZ is filed "% Jeffery M. Bradshaw," using the address and phone number of Bradshaw Smith & Co — Bradshaw's own private CPA practice, not a CAMS office. He's held the Treasurer role since co-founding the organization. That means CAMS's federal financial reporting runs through one board member's own accounting firm, which makes the three-figure discrepancy above a narrower, more answerable question than it might otherwise be: the person best positioned to reconcile it is already on the board.

Separately, 2024 spending was dominated almost entirely by a single line: $347,465.54 for "Main Street Beautification," about 90% of that year's budget, with no itemization available anywhere public and no board minutes on file for the year it occurred. The following year, that same line fell roughly 99%, while total income fell roughly 73% — a pattern that reads as a single large, unexplained project rather than routine year-to-year spending.

A nonprofit that describes its own advocacy

CAMS's January 2024 blog post, "CAMS Advocacy Initiatives," states plainly: "Advocacy is at the core of our mission... engag[ing] in dialogues with local policymakers to influence decisions impacting Main Street." That post predates CAMS's 501(c)(3) conversion, which took effect Nov. 22, 2024 — meaning it describes CAMS's own self-understanding as an advocacy organization from before, not after, its current tax status began.

The rule CAMS is required to follow right now, as of Nov. 22, 2024, is the 501(c)(3) standard — not the looser rules that applied to the 501(c)(6) classification it says it held before. Specifically: lobbying cannot be a "substantial part" of its activities (the IRS has never set an exact percentage, but a 1955 court case treated roughly 5% of an organization's time and effort as the outer edge of "insubstantial"), and it is absolutely barred from supporting or opposing any candidate or political party, in any form. A 501(c)(6) business league — what CAMS says it operated as for most of its earlier history — was never subject to either of those restrictions in the same way. Violating them can cost an organization its tax-exempt status outright.

So the plain answer to "are they allowed to do politics": no campaign involvement of any kind, ever, under their current status — and only a small, non-dominant amount of legislative lobbying. What isn't established by anything in hand is whether CAMS's "Policy Influence" activity continued, unchanged, after Nov. 22, 2024 took effect. The Jan. 2024 post shows what CAMS considered its own mission before the conversion; it doesn't show what it's actually done since.

Scoring the structure

This scorecard measures oversight and accountability — whether the public can see, question, and check this arrangement. It does not measure whether the flowers get planted or the events happen. Nothing in the record reviewed here suggests CAMS's actual programming has failed; the deliverables list above is real. What follows is a separate question: is anyone positioned to catch it if something goes wrong?

What it would take to run like other Main Street programs

None of this requires CAMS to stop existing or the City to stop funding downtown work. Most Main Street organizations nationally operate with the following as baseline practice — the gap here is between that baseline and what's currently documented, not between CAMS and some impossible standard.

  1. Resolve and maintain state corporate standing. A vendor holding an active public contract should not be delinquent with the state at the same time — this is a compliance step, not a judgment call.
  2. Separate city staff roles from nonprofit board seats. A City Manager or other city employee negotiating, approving, and signing a contract with an organization they also govern is the single largest structural gap here. Most Main Street models keep the city's role advisory or funding-only, without a dual seat.
  3. Competitive procurement for a recurring contract. Sole-source renewal, year after year, without competitive bidding, is the exception nationally, not the rule, for an ongoing public contract of this size.
  4. One reconciled set of books. CAMS's own documents currently show three different totals for the same fiscal year. A single, itemized, internally consistent financial statement — with large line items like the 2024 Beautification expense actually itemized — is standard nonprofit practice, not an unusual ask.
  5. Board minutes posted on the schedule CAMS's own bylaws already require. The 45-business-day requirement exists on paper; it isn't being met in practice.
  6. Financial administration independent of a board member's own firm, or, at minimum, clear disclosure of the arrangement. A Treasurer's private accounting practice handling the nonprofit's federal filing isn't unheard of for a small organization, but it should be a disclosed, board-acknowledged arrangement rather than something a reader has to piece together from a phone number.
  7. Accurate public labeling of paid staff versus volunteers. A City-funded Executive Director position should not appear under a "volunteer" header.
  8. One board roster that matches across the state filing, the City's records, and CAMS's own website. Right now there are three different lists.

Who's actually supposed to catch this

Every gap on this page has a body that's nominally responsible for catching it. Laid out plainly, most of those checks turn out to be reactive, self-reported, or triggered only by someone filing a complaint — not routine verification.

Overall oversight grade: D−

Not a flat F, because the pieces of a real oversight system technically exist on paper — a corporate registry, an IRS enforcement power, a Council with hire/fire authority, bylaws with a “self-dealing” clause. The grade is this low because every one of them is either reactive (waits for a complaint), automatic-but-toothless (a renewal deadline), or the same party reviewing itself. Nothing in this structure catches a problem before it becomes public — every finding on this page came from an outside person reading the documents, not from any of these mechanisms working as designed.
