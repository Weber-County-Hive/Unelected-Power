Who Decides, Part 3: Who Runs MIDA, and Who Writes Its Rules?

The short, simple version of the Hive's MIDA case file.

What it is

The Military Installation Development Authority, called MIDA, is a state agency. Lawmakers created it in 2007 to build on land at and near military bases, starting at Hill Air Force Base.

Its board is appointed, not elected. The governor picks 5 members. The Senate president and the House speaker pick 1 each.

In MIDA's areas, county and city rules don't apply. MIDA keeps up to 75% of the new property tax money for 25 years, and its board can add 15 more.

Who writes the rules

From 2007 to 2026, lawmakers passed 22 bills that changed MIDA. One senator, Jerry Stevenson, carried 15 of them. He also sits on MIDA's board. MIDA's law doesn't bar that.

Did the changes help the public?

The Hive grades each change on one question: is it good for the public, on openness, money and the environment?

  • Less open: since 2021, MIDA can send records appeals to its own appeals board instead of the state's records office. Since 2022, MIDA doesn't have to physically post meeting notices.
  • More open: since 2024, your property value notice shows what you pay MIDA. Since 2026, MIDA has to report its tax money every year.

What's next

MIDA's board approved the Stratos data center area in Box Elder County in April 2026. Residents filed a referendum petition and lawsuits. The governor called for "major reform." For 2027, two lawmakers have asked for MIDA bills: Sen. Brady Brammer and Rep. Casey Snider. There's no bill text yet. The Hive checks every day and will add them.

Why it matters to you

Once a county or city agrees, MIDA decides how land in its areas is used and keeps part of the new tax money. Voters don't elect its board. The lawmakers who write its rules can sit on that board. Knowing who decides helps you know who to ask.
