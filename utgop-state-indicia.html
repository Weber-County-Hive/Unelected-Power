The State Party Indicia: candidate money in, postage out.

A page from the Utah Republican Party's Operations Manual describes how candidates can send mailers on the party's nonprofit postage permit. This page sets that page next to the U.S. Postal Service's own rules and next to the 2026 state disclosure records for both sides of the transaction.

The short version

  • A page from the Utah Republican Party's Operations Manual, last revised Feb. 7, 2025, lets candidates mail on the party's nonprofit postage permit. The candidate pays the printer and donates the postage "plus an additional amount about 10%" to the party before the mailer is approved.
  • USPS rules say a state party may mail only its own material at nonprofit prices, and must pay the postage "with no reimbursement from the candidates." Money earmarked for one candidate's mailer is treated as the candidate's money.
  • In 2026, Republican candidates and PACs reported paying the party $441,267.67, and the party reported 39 payments to USPS totaling $305,345.98, mostly in the weeks before the June primary and the November general.
  • Rep. Doug Fiefia's report names the program: "Utah Republican Party State Indicia." Rep. Jim Alvey's report lists "State Inicia" with no address, and no business by that name was found.
  • A campaign vendor, Tactical Campaigns LLC, is listed as giving the party $49,185 in the first half of 2026, while candidates and PACs paid it $118,501.77. The manual says the party works with certain mail vendors who "know the process." Whether any vendor money paid for candidates' postage is not shown by the records.
  • No filing on either side uses the word postage. The 10% can't be checked from these records. The Utah Democratic Party reported no USPS payments in 2026.

What the manual page says

The page is headed "State Party Indicia" and is marked "Last Updated 2/7/25." The copy posted publicly carries a "Confidential" watermark. Under the party's bylaws, the Operations Manual must be available to State Central Committee members at party headquarters.

  • When the party subsidizes mailers. Two cases are listed: a general election in support of the Republican nominee, and a Republican primary where one candidate won outright at convention (state or county).
  • What the mailer can't contain. Inaccurate or false information, or an attack on another Republican candidate.
  • The process. The party approves the graphic, which must say "Postage paid for by the Utah Republican Party" with the party's address and permit #1863. The campaign sends the graphic and mail list to the printer and pays the printer for printing.
  • The donation.
"The campaign/candidate needs to donate to the party for the postage plus an additional amount about 10%."
  • Timing. The donation must reach the party before the mailer is authorized. Then "the USPS will automatically withdraw the postage amount from the state acct."

The manual page does not say what the extra 10% is for. This page calls it "the extra amount about 10%," in the manual's own words.

The program is public. In a July 31, 2025 Salt Lake Tribune story, party chair Robert Axson said candidates request the discount through a form that requires a donation to the state party for the total cost of the mailer, and that the discount is open to registered Republicans, including those in nonpartisan races. The party's Candidates page lists "postage discounts for mailers" and a Postage Discount Form.

What USPS rules say

Federal law lets a state committee of any political party mail at nonprofit prices (39 U.S.C. 3626(e)). USPS spells out the limits in its Domestic Mail Manual, section 703.1.6, in Customer Support Ruling PS-055 (updated January 2017), and in Publication 417.

PS-055: a state committee can support candidates "as long as the campaign materials to be mailed at the nonprofit prices are the committee's own and the committee pays the postage with no reimbursement from the candidates or other committees supporting those candidates."

Tap a card to see what each rule says.

Utah has been here before. In 1995, a USPS administrative law judge heard the Postal Service's case against the Utah State Democratic Committee and the Independent Party of Utah over 1992 mailings. USPS sought $299,969.20 from the Democrats over 96 mailings. Neither party disputed that the money ultimately came from candidates. The parties won because the specific rule was then in an unpublished postal manual, and USPS couldn't prove the parties had been given clear notice of it. The rule was added to the Domestic Mail Manual in July 1993.

Under Pub 417 section 4-4, if USPS finds a mailing wasn't eligible, it may bill the organization the difference between nonprofit and regular postage. This page doesn't say any mailing was ineligible. That is USPS's call, and no USPS finding about 2026 was found.

A question the records raise: a campaign vendor paying the party

The manual page ends with a list of "printing and mailing companies that we have auto withdrawal with," and adds: "They know the process." In the copy posted publicly, the company names are blacked out.

That line matters. It means the party works directly with certain vendors on candidate mailers. If a vendor pays the party for a candidate's postage, the money would take a different path through the records than when the candidate pays the party directly.

The question: are any of the vendor's payments to the party made for its candidate clients' postage? The records don't say. A campaign vendor can also give to a party for its own reasons, and the party paid this vendor $1,350 for services. Nothing here shows wrongdoing by the vendor, the candidates or the party.

Why it matters if the answer is yes

  • Voters couldn't trace it. Someone reading a candidate's report would see a payment to a consultant, not to the party, and couldn't tell the party's postage permit was used.
  • The party's report would name the vendor as the giver, not the candidate whose mailer it was.
  • Under USPS ruling PS-055, money earmarked for one candidate's mailer is the candidate's money no matter who hands it over. Passing it through a vendor wouldn't change how USPS treats it.
  • Under state disclosure, it raises the question of who the contribution is really from, which is a question for the Lieutenant Governor's elections office.

What would settle it

  • The blacked-out list of "printing and mailing companies" on the manual page.
  • The purpose the party records for each vendor contribution, and whether any are tied to a candidate.
  • The Audit Committee's review of those records, which it can request under bylaws 2.4 B.

How the two sides differ

Republican records

  • Candidate and PAC money went to the party in the same weeks the party paid USPS.
  • One payee line names the program: "State Indicia."
  • The candidate gets nonprofit postage rates. In the 1992 Utah case, the nonprofit rate was about 45% lower than the regular bulk rate; today's gap wasn't checked.
  • The party gets the extra amount (about 10%, per the manual) and its name on the mail.
  • In a primary, convention finalists can use it; signature-only candidates can't (bylaws 8.3).

Democratic records

  • Candidate and group money also went to the party, heavily in October.
  • No payee line names a postage program.
  • The state party reported no USPS payments, so the records don't tie these payments to postage.
  • What the money bought (staff, canvassing, data, ads) isn't stated.

Under the law, neither party has an advantage: any state party committee can hold a nonprofit permit, and the Utah Democratic Party used theirs this way in 1992. In the 2026 records, only the Republican side shows candidate money in and party postage out.

What isn't known

Which payments were for postage

No 2026 line on either side says "postage." Only Fiefia's payee names the program.

Whether the 10% was charged

Candidates pay on the printer's estimate and USPS draws the actual postage, so single payments don't line up. The party's own mail also runs through the same permit. The totals can't prove or disprove the 10%.

A test, where same-day amounts exist: Fiefia paid $4,865.00 on May 19, and the party paid USPS $4,738.29 that day, 2.7% more. Teuscher's $6,390.63 vs. the party's $6,390.89 (next item) is essentially 0%. Neither pairing is proven, but neither shows 10%.

Two Tran entries of $4,310.00 on the same day

The party's report lists two $4,310.00 entries from "Vote Tami Tran" on May 28. That may be two mailers, or one entry recorded twice.

Stuart Adams for Senate's payments to the party

The Hive's tracker of the party's Primary report lists five transfers of about $7,800 each on May 20, totaling either $31,375 or $39,175; the tracker gives both. Adams Leadership PAC also paid the party $48,603.73 the same day. What they were for isn't stated.

A near-match: $6,390.89 and $6,390.63

The party paid USPS $6,390.89 on May 28. Rep. Jordan Teuscher paid the party $6,390.63 on June 1, labeled "Supplies." They're 26 cents apart. Nothing on either report ties them together, so this is not a finding.

Two identical USPS payments

The party paid USPS $4,793.24 on Oct. 6 and again on Oct. 7. That may be two mailings, or one payment entered twice.

Whether the manual page was approved

No State Central Committee minutes found show approval or ratification of the Feb. 7, 2025 revision.

The party's own rules

  • Bylaws 5.0 (amended Nov. 15, 2025). The State Party Officers keep a written Operations Manual, available to State Central Committee members at headquarters. The State Executive Committee reviews it the same year after each nominating convention. Changes go to the Executive Committee for review and to the State Central Committee for approval. New policies adopted in between must be ratified at the next State Central Committee meeting.
  • Bylaws 2.4 B. The Audit Committee has access, by request approved by a majority of the committee, to the party's financial documents and reports, shared only with State Central Committee members.
  • Bylaws 8.3 A. Party officers and staff give equal access to party services to all Republican candidates, except those who reached the primary without going through convention.
  • Sept. 6, 2025. The State Central Committee passed an "Amendment to Update Operations Manual Timeline," 118 yes, 12 no, 8 present. The minutes don't give its text.

Questions to ask, and whom to ask

  • The Lieutenant Governor's elections office: When a candidate mails on a party's nonprofit permit, should the difference between regular and nonprofit postage be reported as an in-kind contribution from the party? Should payments for postage be labeled as postage?
  • Your State Central Committee members: When was the State Party Indicia section approved or ratified? Does it match bylaws 8.3, which list more candidates than the manual does?
  • The Utah Republican Party Audit Committee: Has it requested the records for the State Indicia donations and the party's USPS payments under bylaws 2.4 B?
  • The Utah Republican Party Audit Committee: Which printing and mailing companies are on the manual's list, and were any contributions from those companies made for a candidate's mailer?
  • The Lieutenant Governor's elections office: If a vendor pays a party for a candidate's postage, whose contribution is it, and how should each side report it?
  • USPS Mailing Standards: Does a candidate donation made under this process, before the party approves that candidate's mailer, meet ruling PS-055?
