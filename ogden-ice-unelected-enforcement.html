The Officer Who Wasn't Elected, The Agent Who Never Will Be

On Aug. 25, 2026, an Ogden man was detained by ICE while a city police officer stood by. The mayor and council who answer to voters spent the next three weeks explaining a decision neither of them made — because the people who made it don't answer to Ogden voters at all.

The Weber County Hive · Public records only · No comment requested
Click to switch between the full case file and a plain-language version of every section.

Every account of what happened on Aug. 25 traces back to two people: an Ogden police officer, and a federal immigration agent. Neither was elected. Neither can be voted out. And the officials who can be voted out — Mayor Ben Nadolski, the Ogden City Council — spent the following weeks defending, explaining, and in Nadolski's case declining to further constrain, a decision that was never theirs to make in the moment it was made.

That gap — between who answers to the public and who exercises power over it — is the subject of this file.

In plain words

Two people decided what happened on Aug. 25: a police officer and an ICE agent. Nobody voted for either one of them, and nobody can vote either one of them out of a job. The mayor and the city council were voted in by Ogden residents — but they weren't there that day, and they didn't make the call. They just had to answer questions about it for weeks afterward.

This whole page is about that gap: the people we can vote for aren't always the people making the decisions that affect us.

The mayor's own framing, given to the Standard-Examiner on Sept. 10, makes the point without meaning to: "Officer discretion in that moment is there for a reason... no set of policies could ever dictate how an officer is to respond in every single circumstance." That is a description of unreviewable, case-by-case power — exercised, in this instance, by someone no one in Ogden voted for, in an encounter with someone no one in Utah has any electoral leverage over whatsoever.

In plain words

The mayor himself said, basically: "Officers just have to use their own judgment — we can't write a rule for every situation." That means, in the moment, the officer got to decide everything. Nobody elected had a say. And the other person in the encounter — the ICE agent — can't be voted out by anyone in Utah, period.

The Account That Doesn't Quite Add Up

OPD's public defense of the officer rests on one specific claim: that he refused to help before any of this reached video, and the ICE agent simply drove off. That claim traces to one place — OPD itself, as relayed by KUTV. It is not confirmed by Councilman Lundell's video, not confirmed by any other outlet's independent reporting, and not shown in the portion of bodycam footage the Hive has reviewed. It is the department's own account of its own conduct.

What's actually on camera sits awkwardly next to that account. If the officer had already refused, flatly, to have any part in stopping this driver, there's little reason for him to walk toward the ICE agent's vehicle afterward, on his own initiative, and ask — on tape — whether the agent has "a reason to stop them." That is a question about legal justification for the exact stop OPD says he'd already turned down. He then tells the agent he'll stay and "make sure that [nothing goes south]... but I can't help" — the language of someone weighing how far to go in the moment, not someone who had already settled the question minutes earlier.

Also missing from every account, official or otherwise: any indication the officer told the driver — who approached him separately, on his own, scared that a stranger was following him — what the officer already knew, if OPD's version is accurate: that this was the same vehicle ICE had already asked him to help stop.

None of this proves OPD's account is false. It's possible the officer declined once, off camera, and then revisited the question aloud as the situation developed. But a police department's own description of its own conduct, when the parts that did reach camera don't obviously square with it, is not the same thing as a verified account. Readers weighing how much to trust Ogden's public statements about ICE cooperation — including the "officer discretion" defense examined throughout this file — should know that distinction exists.

In plain words

OPD says the officer told ICE "no" right away, and the agent just drove off on his own. But that's the department talking about itself — nobody else has confirmed it, and it's not in the video clip that's been reviewed here.

Here's what doesn't quite add up: if the officer had already said "no" for good, why does he later walk up to the ICE agent and ask, "do you have a reason to stop them?" That's a strange question to ask if you've already refused to help with exactly that. Then he tells the agent he'll stick around and "make sure nothing goes south" — which sounds more like someone deciding in the moment, not someone who'd already made up his mind.

Also missing: any sign the officer told the driver — who came to him, scared, asking for help — what he already knew, if OPD's story is true: that this exact car was the one ICE had already asked about.

This doesn't prove OPD made anything up. Maybe the officer really did say no once, off camera, and then thought it over out loud later. But when a police department's own story about itself doesn't quite match what showed up on camera, that's worth knowing before deciding how much to trust everything else they've said.

The Floor, The Ceiling, and What Actually Happened

There's already a law on the books — it just doesn't do what people assume

Utah Code §76-14-205 is already in force, right now, independent of anything that happened in 2026. It says a city or county may not adopt any ordinance, regulation, or policy that restricts its own law enforcement agency from cooperating with federal immigration authorities. That's a floor: a legal minimum of cooperation no city is allowed to go below. It does not, by itself, require any officer to do anything.

Separately, HB571 (Rep. Trevor Lee) tried to build a ceiling on top of that floor in the 2026 session — an affirmative "best efforts" mandate requiring agencies to actively support federal immigration enforcement, backed by attorney general lawsuits, court injunctions, and civil fines up to $5,000 against individual officials. That part died. It never received a hearing in either chamber; the House struck its enacting clause and filed it as a bill not passed on March 6, 2026.

In plain words

Utah already has a rule: cities can't tell their own police to never help ICE. That's the only thing the rule does — it stops cities from banning cooperation. It does NOT force any officer to help ICE. Think of it as a floor: cities can't go below it, but nothing forces them to go above it either.

Someone in the Legislature tried to pass a second, tougher rule this year that WOULD have forced cities to actively help ICE, with big fines for cities that didn't. That second rule never passed. It's dead. It never even got a hearing.

In plain terms

Picture a store owner who decides, in the moment, not to volunteer information to a bill collector who calls asking about a customer. Nothing requires her to answer, so declining that one call breaks no rule.

Now picture her posting a sign in the window: "Official store policy — we never give bill collectors any information, ever." If there's a law barring stores from having that kind of standing policy, the sign itself is the violation — even though the underlying behavior, declining one phone call, was perfectly legal a moment ago.

That's the exact gap in Ogden. The officer declining ICE's request for a traffic stop on Aug. 25 was the phone call — a one-time judgment call, fully legal, because nothing requires him to say yes. What the council tried to write in its "Affirming Community Trust" resolution was the sign in the window — a permanent, written policy binding every officer, every time. Utah law doesn't touch the phone call. It already forbids the sign.

What's legal, and what would get the city in trouble

No trouble

An individual officer declining, in the moment, to actively volunteer help — like the Ogden officer refusing ICE's request for a traffic stop on Aug. 25. Nothing in current law requires an officer to affirmatively assist ICE. Case-by-case, officer-by-officer non-cooperation carries zero legal risk.

One officer deciding, just this one time, not to help ICE = totally fine. No rule broken.

Legal exposure

The city adopting a formal written policy, ordinance, or directive telling officers to never cooperate with ICE — the kind of blanket ban the council's original resolution language edged toward. That would violate the existing §76-14-205 floor, regardless of whether HB571's specific penalty scheme ever became law.

The whole city writing down "we will never help ICE" as an official rule = against the law.

That distinction is the actual reason nothing was ever formalized in writing over nearly two years of trying — not because Nadolski changed his mind, but because the one thing residents kept asking for (a written guarantee) is the one thing the city's own lawyers say state law won't let it put on paper.

In plain words

That's exactly why nothing has ever been put in writing, even after two years of people asking. Not because the mayor changed his mind — because his own lawyers say the law won't let the city write down the exact promise people keep asking for.

ICE's own authority is narrower than the ask suggests

Federal immigration agents have no statutory authority to enforce state traffic law. A civil traffic stop — the kind the agent asked the Ogden officer to perform — is a power that belongs to state and local police, not ICE. ICE's own authority is limited to stopping and questioning someone where it has reasonable suspicion of an immigration violation, arresting with probable cause of one, executing a warrant, or engaging in a genuinely consensual encounter. That gap in ICE's own legal toolkit is very likely why the agent needed the officer's help in the first place — and why, when the officer declined, the agent proceeded on a wholly separate legal basis that required no cooperation from Ogden at all.

In plain words

Here's a secret: ICE agents legally cannot do a regular traffic stop — that's only allowed for real police. ICE can only stop someone if they already have a good reason to suspect an immigration problem, have a warrant, or the person agrees to talk. That's probably exactly why this agent needed the officer's help in the first place. And once the officer said no, the agent still found a completely different, legal way to make the arrest on his own — no help from Ogden needed.

The thing nobody tried, that the law never stopped them from trying

Residents have asked Ogden City Council, at nearly every meeting on this subject since January, for a written guarantee of how officers will handle these encounters. The city's answer has consistently been that state law won't let it put anything in writing. That's true of one specific kind of writing — a policy that restricts cooperation with ICE. It is not true of writing that simply states the law and the discretion officers already have, in both directions, without directing anyone toward either one.

A plain-language explainer — officers are not required to assist ICE with civil immigration enforcement, and are not prohibited from doing so either; the choice is left to individual discretion in each encounter — commits the city to nothing, expands no one's authority, and restricts no one's cooperation. It doesn't tell an officer what to do. It tells the public, and the officers themselves, what the law already says. That appears to fall entirely outside what §76-14-205 forbids, because nothing about it reduces cooperation below whatever the law already allows.

This is different from what Weber County Sheriff Arbon signed. His 287(g) agreement with ICE is a voluntary federal contract that actively expands what his deputies can do — deputizing them, after training, to exercise real federal immigration authority in the field. Nothing in §76-14-205 required that, and nothing in it would require Ogden to match it. The statute is a floor imposed on every city and county in Utah regardless of what they want; 287(g) is a door one elected sheriff chose, on his own, to walk through. Ogden could publish a plain-language explainer of existing discretion without coming one inch closer to a 287(g)-style agreement — the two are not points on the same scale.

Nearly two years into this conversation, the city has not published that explainer. Not because the law forbids it. Because, as far as the public record shows, nobody has tried.

And that's the part worth sitting with: the identical state floor sits under both governments, and each one's elected leadership did something different with the room above it. Weber County's sheriff, on his own initiative, walked through the door and formally expanded his deputies' role in federal immigration enforcement — a choice he made publicly, and one Weber County voters can credit or hold him accountable for at the ballot box. Ogden's mayor and council have done the opposite in public statements — Nadolski has repeatedly resisted formalizing any cooperation arrangement and the city has never pursued a 287(g)-style agreement of its own. Neither choice was compelled by §76-14-205; the statute is silent on whether any agency does more than the floor requires. What separates the two governments isn't the law. It's what each set of elected officials chose to do with the discretion the law left them — and only one of them has ever put that choice in writing.

In plain words

People keep asking Ogden to write down a promise about ICE. The city keeps saying "the law won't let us write anything down." But that's only half true — the law only stops the city from writing a rule that BANS helping ICE. The city could still write something that just explains the law, without banning or requiring anything. Nobody has tried that yet.

Imagine a simple flyer that says: "Officers don't have to help ICE, and they're also not banned from helping ICE — it's up to them, each time." That flyer doesn't create any new rule. It doesn't stop anyone from doing anything. It just tells people the truth about what's already allowed. That looks like it would be completely legal to publish.

This is different from what the county sheriff did. He signed a real agreement with ICE that gives his deputies brand-new power to act like immigration agents. That's a much bigger step than a plain explainer flyer. Ogden could publish the flyer without coming anywhere near what the sheriff signed — they're not even on the same scale.

Nobody has published that flyer in almost two years. Not because it's against the law. Just because nobody's tried.

Here's the interesting part: the exact same state law applies to both governments. But the sheriff chose to sign up for MORE cooperation with ICE, out loud, in writing. Ogden's mayor has said the opposite in public — he doesn't want to formalize any of it. Neither choice was forced by the law. It's just what each elected person decided to do with the choice the law left open — and only one of them ever wrote it down.

The Discretion Problem

Both Nadolski and Sube, in their Sept. 10 interview, defended the absence of a written policy specifically on the grounds that officer discretion needs to stay unwritten and case-by-case. Sube: "I can't just arbitrarily make decisions... They don't put me in here to become the judge, jury and executioner in every scenario." That may be true of any one incident. But the accumulation of it — discretion exercised by an appointed chief, applied by an un-elected officer, in an encounter with a federal agent no one in Utah can vote out — is a description of where the actual decision-making power in this story sits. It isn't with anyone Ogden residents put in office.

In plain words

Both the mayor and the police chief said basically the same thing: "We can't write a rule for every possible situation — officers have to use their own judgment." That might be true for any one single moment. But zoom out: the chief wasn't elected, the officer wasn't elected, and the ICE agent definitely wasn't elected. So the real power in this whole story sat with three people that nobody in Ogden ever got to vote for.
